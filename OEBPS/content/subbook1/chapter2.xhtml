Chapter 2: Solar Panel Technologies

Solar photovoltaic technology has evolved dramatically since the first practical silicon cells were developed in the 1950s. Today, a diverse range of PV technologies exists, each with unique characteristics, advantages, and ideal applications. This chapter surveys the major solar panel technologies, from well-established crystalline silicon to emerging thin-film and next-generation approaches.

2.1 Monocrystalline Silicon

Monocrystalline silicon (mono-Si) solar cells are made from a single, continuous crystal structure. This technology currently dominates the global PV market due to its high efficiency and proven reliability.

Manufacturing Process

The production of monocrystalline silicon cells begins with electronic-grade silicon (EG-Si), which is purified to 99.9999999% (9N) purity. The most common method for growing single-crystal silicon is the Czochralski process:

  1. Electronic-grade silicon is melted in a quartz crucible at approximately 1420°C.
  2. A precisely oriented seed crystal is dipped into the molten silicon and slowly withdrawn while being rotated.
  3. As the seed is withdrawn, the molten silicon solidifies onto the seed, maintaining the single-crystal structure.
  4. The resulting ingot, typically 200-450 mm in diameter and several meters long, is then sliced into thin wafers (150-200 µm thick).

Alternative methods include the float-zone (FZ) process, which produces even higher purity silicon without a crucible, though at higher cost.

Cell Structure and Design

Monocrystalline silicon solar cells typically have the following structure:

The front surface of the cell features:

  • Anti-reflection coating (ARC): Typically silicon nitride (SiNx) with a refractive index of ~2.0, designed to minimize reflection losses across the solar spectrum.
  • Front contact grid: Silver (Ag) finger electrodes that collect current while minimizing shading of the cell surface. Typical grid patterns use 3-5 busbars with numerous finer fingers.
  • Emitter: Heavily doped n+ layer (phosphorus-doped) that forms the p-n junction with the p-type base.
  • Base: p-type silicon substrate (boron-doped) that forms the bulk of the cell.
  • Back contact: Aluminum (Al) layer that covers the entire rear surface, providing good electrical contact and reflecting unabsorbed light back into the cell (back surface field effect).

2.2 Polycrystalline Silicon

Polycrystalline silicon (poly-Si) cells, also known as multicrystalline silicon, are made from multiple silicon crystals rather than a single crystal. This technology offers a more economical alternative to monocrystalline silicon with slightly lower efficiency.

Manufacturing Process

The production of polycrystalline silicon typically uses the casting method:

  1. Electronic-grade silicon is melted in a quartz crucible.
  2. The molten silicon is directionally solidified, with a temperature gradient causing crystal growth from the bottom up.
  3. Controlled cooling produces a block of silicon with multiple columnar crystals, typically several centimeters across.
  4. The solidified block is then cut into bricks and sliced into wafers.

Unlike the Czochralski process, casting does not require a seed crystal and can produce larger ingots (up to 1 m × 1 m in cross-section), reducing material costs.

2.3 Thin-Film Technologies

Thin-film solar cells use layers of photovoltaic material only a few micrometers thick, deposited on a substrate such as glass, metal, or plastic. This approach significantly reduces material usage and manufacturing costs compared to wafer-based crystalline silicon technologies.

The three main types of thin-film PV technologies are:

Amorphous Silicon (a-Si)

Amorphous silicon (a-Si) is a non-crystalline form of silicon where the atoms lack a periodic structure. Unlike crystalline silicon, a-Si can be deposited in thin layers using processes such as plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD).

Advantages:

  • Can be deposited on flexible substrates
  • Lower temperature coefficient than c-Si
  • Performs better in low-light conditions
  • Lower manufacturing cost

Disadvantages:

  • Lower efficiency (6-10%)
  • Degradation under light exposure (Staebler-Wronski effect)
  • Larger area required for same power output

Cadmium Telluride (CdTe)

Cadmium telluride (CdTe) is a compound semiconductor with a direct band gap of approximately 1.44 eV, which is close to the optimal value for solar energy conversion. CdTe cells are typically produced using a sequence of deposition processes:

  1. A thin layer of cadmium sulfide (CdS) is deposited on a glass substrate coated with transparent conducting oxide (TCO).
  2. The CdTe layer is deposited using methods such as close-spaced sublimation (CSS) or vapor transport deposition (VTD).
  3. A post-deposition treatment with cadmium chloride (CdCl2) improves the material properties.
  4. Back contacts and interconnections are added to complete the module.

Advantages:

  • High absorption coefficient (only ~1 µm thick layer needed)
  • Module efficiencies exceeding 22% (First Solar record: 22.8% in 2022)
  • Low manufacturing cost
  • Scalable production
  • Good performance in high-temperature and low-light conditions

Disadvantages:

  • Use of cadmium (a heavy metal) and tellurium (a rare element)
  • Recycling considerations for end-of-life modules

Copper Indium Gallium Selenide (CIGS)

CIGS cells use a compound semiconductor from the chalcopyrite family with the chemical formula CuInxGa(1-x)Se2. The band gap can be engineered by varying the In/Ga ratio, typically between 1.0 and 1.7 eV.

CIGS deposition is typically performed using co-evaporation or sputtering followed by selenization. The process allows for flexible substrate materials, including polymer films.

Advantages:

  • Highest efficiency among thin-film technologies (23.35% cell efficiency record)
  • Flexible and lightweight modules possible
  • Excellent performance in low-light conditions
  • Low temperature coefficient

Disadvantages:

  • Complex manufacturing process
  • Use of indium (relatively rare) and gallium
  • Difficulty in uniform large-area deposition

2.4 Perovskite Cells

Perovskite solar cells represent one of the most exciting developments in PV technology in recent years. Named after the mineral perovskite (CaTiO3), these cells use organic-inorganic hybrid compounds with the general formula ABX3, where A and B are cations and X is an anion.

The most commonly studied perovskite material for PV applications is methylammonium lead iodide (CH3NH3PbI3), which has a band gap of approximately 1.55 eV—close to the optimal value for solar energy conversion.

Advantages:

  • High absorption coefficient (only ~0.5 µm thick layer needed)
  • Rapid efficiency improvements (from 3.8% in 2009 to 25.7% in 2023)
  • Tunable band gap through composition engineering
  • Potential for low-cost manufacturing (solution processing)
  • Compatibility with flexible substrates
  • Potential for multi-junction applications (tandem cells)

Challenges:

  • Stability: Sensitivity to moisture, oxygen, UV light, and temperature
  • Lead content: Most high-efficiency perovskites contain lead, raising environmental and toxicity concerns
  • Hysteresis: Anomalous behavior in current-voltage measurements that complicates characterization
  • Scalability: Difficulty in producing uniform, large-area films
  • Lifetime: Current devices have demonstrated lifetimes of hundreds to thousands of hours, far below the 25+ years required for commercial viability

Despite these challenges, perovskite technology is considered one of the most promising pathways to next-generation high-efficiency, low-cost PV.

Perovskite-Silicon Tandem Cells

One of the most promising applications for perovskite cells is in tandem configurations with silicon cells. In a tandem (or multi-junction) cell, different PV materials are stacked to capture different portions of the solar spectrum more efficiently.

In a perovskite-silicon tandem:

  • The perovskite cell (with a band gap of ~1.6-1.8 eV) captures high-energy photons in the blue and green portions of the spectrum.
  • The silicon cell (with a band gap of 1.12 eV) captures the remaining lower-energy photons in the red and infrared portions.
  • The two cells are connected in series, with the total voltage being the sum of the individual cell voltages.

Theoretical efficiency for such a tandem exceeds 40%, and laboratory devices have already demonstrated efficiencies over 33%, surpassing the efficiency of single-junction silicon cells.

Theoretical efficiency limit for perovskite-silicon tandem: ~43%

Current record (2025): 33.9% (Oxford PV)

2.5 Bifacial Panels

Bifacial solar panels are designed to capture sunlight from both the front and rear surfaces of the module. This is achieved through several key design features:

  • Double-glass construction: Both front and rear surfaces use tempered glass instead of the traditional backsheet.
  • Transparent back contact: The rear electrical contacts are made with transparent or semi-transparent materials.
  • Symmetric or asymmetric cell design: The solar cells themselves may be designed to collect light from both sides (bifacial cells) or the module may use conventional monofacial cells with a transparent rear surface.

The additional energy gain from the rear surface depends on several factors:

  • Albedo: The reflectivity of the ground surface. Snow, sand, and concrete have high albedo (70-90%), while grass and asphalt have lower albedo (20-30%).
  • Mounting height: Higher mounting allows more reflected light to reach the rear surface.
  • Row spacing: Adequate spacing between rows prevents shading of the rear surfaces.
  • Module design: The bifaciality factor (ratio of rear to front efficiency) of the module.

Typical bifacial gain (the percentage increase in energy yield compared to monofacial modules) ranges from 5% to 20%, depending on these factors. In optimal conditions with high albedo and proper system design, bifacial gains can exceed 30%.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has surveyed the major solar panel technologies, each with unique characteristics and applications:

  • Monocrystalline silicon: Highest efficiency, most established technology, dominant in the market.
  • Polycrystalline silicon: Lower cost alternative with slightly lower efficiency, still widely used.
  • Thin-film technologies: a-Si, CdTe, and CIGS offer lower material usage and manufacturing costs, with varying efficiencies and market presence.
  • Perovskite: Rapidly advancing technology with high efficiency potential, particularly in tandem configurations with silicon.
  • Bifacial: Panels that capture light from both sides, providing additional energy yield in appropriate installations.

The choice of technology depends on factors such as cost, efficiency requirements, installation environment, and specific application needs. Crystalline silicon (both mono and poly) currently dominates the market, but thin-film and tandem technologies are gaining ground in specific niches and for future applications.

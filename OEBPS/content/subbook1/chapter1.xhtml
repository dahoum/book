Chapter 1: Photovoltaic Principles

At the heart of every solar cell lies the photovoltaic effect—a quantum mechanical process that enables the direct conversion of light into electricity. This chapter explores the fundamental physics behind this remarkable phenomenon, from the behavior of light to the properties of semiconductor materials that make modern photovoltaics possible.

1.1 Semiconductor Physics

To understand how photovoltaic cells work, we must first examine the properties of semiconductor materials. Semiconductors occupy a middle ground between conductors (like metals) and insulators (like glass). Their unique electronic properties can be precisely controlled through a process called doping.

Atomic Structure and Energy Bands

In any material, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. In isolated atoms, these are sharp, well-defined levels. However, when atoms come together to form a solid, these individual energy levels broaden into energy bands.

The two most important energy bands for semiconductor behavior are:

Valence band
The highest energy band that is completely or nearly completely filled with electrons at absolute zero temperature. Electrons in this band are bound to their parent atoms.
Conduction band
The lowest energy band that is empty (or nearly empty) at absolute zero. Electrons in this band are free to move throughout the material, enabling electrical conduction.
Band gap (Eg)
The energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band. In semiconductors, this gap is typically between 0.1 eV and 4 eV.

For silicon (Si), the most common PV material:

Eg,Si = 1.12 eV at 300 K

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

A pure semiconductor with no impurities or defects is called an intrinsic semiconductor. At temperatures above absolute zero, thermal energy can excite some electrons from the valence band to the conduction band, creating free electrons and leaving behind positively charged holes in the valence band. Both electrons and holes can contribute to electrical conduction.

The number of free electrons (and holes) in an intrinsic semiconductor is given by the intrinsic carrier concentration (ni):

ni = √(NC NV) exp(-Eg / (2 k T))

where NC and NV are the effective density of states in the conduction and valence bands, k is Boltzmann's constant (8.617×10-5 eV/K), and T is temperature in Kelvin.

For silicon at room temperature (300 K), ni ≈ 1.0 × 1010 cm-3.

An extrinsic semiconductor is one that has been intentionally doped with small amounts of impurities to change its electrical properties. There are two types of doping:

Common Dopants for Silicon
Type Dopant Element Valence Electrons Effect
N-type Phosphorus (P) 5 Donor - provides extra electrons
N-type Arsenic (As) 5 Donor - provides extra electrons
P-type Boron (B) 3 Acceptor - creates holes

1.2 Solar Spectrum

Sunlight reaching Earth's surface consists of electromagnetic radiation across a range of wavelengths. The solar spectrum is approximately that of a blackbody at 5778 K (the temperature of the Sun's photosphere), modified by absorption in Earth's atmosphere.

The most important parameter for PV applications is the solar irradiance—the power per unit area received from the sun. At Earth's surface, under clear sky conditions, the maximum solar irradiance is approximately 1000 W/m² (known as one sun).

The solar constant (GSC)—the extraterrestrial solar irradiance at the mean Earth-Sun distance—is approximately:

GSC = 1367 W/m²

About 30% of this energy is reflected or absorbed by Earth's atmosphere under clear sky conditions, resulting in the ~1000 W/m² at the surface.

Photon Energy and Band Gap Matching

A photon of light with wavelength λ has energy E given by Planck's equation:

E = h c / λ

where h is Planck's constant (6.626×10-34 J·s) and c is the speed of light (2.998×108 m/s).

In convenient units: E (eV) = 1240 / λ (nm)

For a photon to be absorbed and contribute to electrical power generation, its energy must be greater than or equal to the band gap energy of the semiconductor:

Ephoton ≥ Eg

If Ephoton < Eg, the photon passes through the material without being absorbed. If Ephoton > Eg, the excess energy (Ephoton - Eg) is typically lost as heat.

Example: Photon Energy Calculation

Calculate the energy of a photon with a wavelength of 500 nm. Is this photon capable of being absorbed by silicon (Eg = 1.12 eV)?

Solution:
E = 1240 eV·nm / 500 nm = 2.48 eV

Since 2.48 eV > 1.12 eV, the photon can be absorbed by silicon. The excess energy (2.48 - 1.12 = 1.36 eV) will be lost as heat.

1.3 PV Cell Operation

A photovoltaic cell converts light into electricity through a multi-step process that occurs within a p-n junction.

The P-N Junction

A p-n junction is formed when n-type and p-type semiconductor materials are brought into intimate contact. At the junction, electrons from the n-side diffuse to the p-side, and holes from the p-side diffuse to the n-side. This creates a region depleted of free charge carriers called the depletion region.

The diffusion of charge carriers creates a built-in electric field across the junction, with the n-side having a positive charge (due to ionized donors) and the p-side having a negative charge (due to ionized acceptors). This electric field establishes a built-in potential (Vbi) that opposes further diffusion.

Vbi = (k T / q) ln(NA ND / ni2)

where NA and ND are the acceptor and donor concentrations, respectively, and q is the elementary charge (1.602×10-19 C).

Photon Absorption and Charge Generation

When a photon with energy greater than Eg is absorbed in the semiconductor:

  1. An electron is excited from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving behind a hole in the valence band.
  2. The electron-hole pair (EHP) is created. The pair will eventually recombine unless the carriers are separated.
  3. In a p-n junction, the built-in electric field separates the electron (which moves to the n-side) and the hole (which moves to the p-side).
  4. If the p-n junction is connected to an external circuit, the separated charges can flow through the circuit, creating a current.

This process is the fundamental mechanism of photovoltaic conversion.

Carrier Separation and Collection

Not all absorbed photons result in useful electrical power. Several loss mechanisms reduce the efficiency of PV cells:

  • Recombination: Electron-hole pairs may recombine before being separated by the junction field. Recombination can occur through various mechanisms including radiative, Shockley-Read-Hall, and Auger processes.
  • Photons with insufficient energy: Photons with energy less than Eg are not absorbed.
  • Excess photon energy: Photons with energy greater than Eg lose the excess energy as heat (thermalization).
  • Resistive losses: Current flowing through the semiconductor and external circuit encounters resistance, resulting in I2R losses.
  • Reflection: Some incident light is reflected from the cell surface and not absorbed.

1.4 Efficiency Limits

The power conversion efficiency (η) of a PV cell is defined as the ratio of electrical power output to incident optical power input:

η = (Pout / Pin) × 100%

where Pout is the maximum electrical power output and Pin is the incident optical power.

The Shockley-Queisser Limit

In 1961, William Shockley and Hans Queisser calculated the theoretical maximum efficiency of a single-junction PV cell under unconcentrated sunlight. This Shockley-Queisser limit (also called the detailed balance limit) considers:

  1. The solar spectrum as a blackbody at 5778 K
  2. Radiative recombination as the only loss mechanism
  3. Infinite mobility of charge carriers (no resistive losses)
  4. No reflection losses

The Shockley-Queisser analysis shows that for an optimal band gap of ~1.34 eV, the maximum theoretical efficiency is approximately 33.7%. Real PV cells have efficiencies below this limit due to additional loss mechanisms not considered in the ideal case.

For silicon (Eg = 1.12 eV), the Shockley-Queisser limit is approximately 29%. The current world record efficiency for silicon solar cells is 26.8% (Kaneka Corporation, 2022), approaching this theoretical limit.

Overcoming the Shockley-Queisser Limit

Several approaches can exceed the Shockley-Queisser limit for single-junction cells:

Multi-junction cells
Stack multiple PV cells with different band gaps, each optimized for a different portion of the solar spectrum. Theoretical efficiency under concentrated sunlight exceeds 86% for an infinite stack.
Hot carrier cells
Harvest the excess energy of hot carriers (electrons and holes with energy greater than Eg) before they thermalize, potentially adding several percentage points to efficiency.
Multi-exciton generation
In some materials (particularly quantum dots), a single high-energy photon can generate multiple electron-hole pairs, increasing the quantum efficiency above 100%.
Up- and down-conversion
Convert photons with energy below Eg to higher energy photons that can be absorbed (up-conversion), or split high-energy photons into multiple lower-energy photons (down-conversion).
Practical Considerations

While these approaches can theoretically exceed the Shockley-Queisser limit, they introduce additional complexity and cost. Most commercial PV systems continue to use single-junction silicon cells, which offer the best combination of efficiency, reliability, and cost-effectiveness for terrestrial applications.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has introduced the fundamental physics of photovoltaic energy conversion:

  • Semiconductor materials have energy bands separated by a band gap (Eg), which determines their electrical properties.
  • Doping with impurities (n-type and p-type) allows control of semiconductor conductivity.
  • The p-n junction creates a built-in electric field that separates photon-generated electron-hole pairs.
  • Photon absorption requires Ephoton ≥ Eg, with excess energy lost as heat.
  • The Shockley-Queisser limit (~33.7%) defines the maximum theoretical efficiency for single-junction PV cells under unconcentrated sunlight.
  • Multiple approaches exist to potentially exceed this limit, though most commercial systems use single-junction silicon cells.
